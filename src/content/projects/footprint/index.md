## The brief

War in Ukraine, political tension and sabotage of supply chains have put food security back at the centre of Sweden's total defence. We set out to find a protein source that works in everyday life *and* in a crisis: no refrigeration, long shelf life, high nutrient density — and something people would actually want to eat.

Our mission statement: create a sustainable, appetising and nutritious protein source, in and out of crisis.

## From gardening to crickets

The pre-study started with home growing; an informal interview with a gardener convinced us it wasn't the right track. A brainstorm pointed to alternative proteins, and insects stood out. Crickets need 1.7 kg of feed per kilo of meat against 5 kg for pork and 10 kg for beef, and emit a fraction of the greenhouse gases. But the insect products on the Swedish market tasted of little but insect, and protein meal replacements were too sweet to live on.

## Four concepts, one matrix

Each of us led one of the project's five phases. I led the ideation phase: structuring the work, setting deadlines and making sure we kept them. The phase opened with a workshop with a group from another field — braindrawing, then open discussion — followed by individual thumbnails.

In concept development we sorted the thumbnails into four concepts — protein powder, protein bar, jerky and an additive — built four prototypes and scored them in a weighted matrix with three external participants from the target group, using the critical functions from the pre-study as criteria. The additive — a bouillon-style puck you crumble into food or eat as it is — won.

## Designing the system, not just the product

Production design was the hardest phase, because neither the product nor its supply chain existed. We compared insect species approved in Sweden on nutrition and life cycle and chose the house cricket, which grows from egg to adult in about two months.

- **Farms.** Refurbished 12-metre containers work as greenhouses for insects, each holding 96–120 breeding boxes of up to 10,000 crickets. Placed about four to a farm, they get feed, power, water and labour locally, and the farmer gets cricket waste back as fertiliser and animal feed.
- **Factory.** A two-day cycle — freezing, sieving, 13 hours of overnight roasting, then milling, mixing, pressing and packing — produces 2,000 tins a day, about 520,000 a year.
- **Packaging.** Waxed carton, chosen in Ansys Granta EduPack for its low climate impact and because it can be composted when the wax is natural.
- **Economics.** A full cost calculation — materials, wages including night-shift supplements, premises and machinery depreciated over seven years — lands at 39 SEK per tin. That is 195 SEK per kilo, against 227–798 SEK for the closest comparable products.

## Crisis mode

When crisis hits, the customer changes from the active individual to MSB, municipalities and the Armed Forces. The line stays the same: the cube press and packing machine are taken out of use, and an add-on on the mill and mixer weighs out 10 kg sacks — 40 a day, each with over 70,000 kcal, enough for the daily needs of about 35 adults. A warehouse that stays closed in normal operation holds the sack materials, the farmers stay on their farms under their wartime assignments, and the factory produces its own power from biogas generators if the grid goes down.

## Result

Digital renders of the tin and the containers, and physical prototypes for the final presentation: the packaging, its labels and a model of the breeding box. I was part of the hands-on work of making the labels and building those prototypes, trying 3D printing, foam and general model making along the way. I also co-wrote all of the project memos.
